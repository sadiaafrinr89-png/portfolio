The business context

Following goods.
Keeping the record clear.

The BRAC Inventory Management System connects purchasing, receiving, and issuing stock in one enterprise web application.

A warehouse transaction carries more than a quantity. It can involve a supplier, a purchase order, a project or department, different units of measure, supporting documents, and an approval decision. The interface needs to keep that context connected as goods move through the operation.

The business need is to make incoming and outgoing stock traceable: what was ordered, what arrived, where it is going, and which actions are still pending. My design scope covered Purchase Orders, Stock In, and Stock Out, with their related forms, review states, and interactions.

Purchase

Record the vendor, ordered items, quantities, prices, and delivery information.

Receive

Capture received quantities and units, documents, and quality-check information.

Issue

Record the destination and recipient, review the transaction, and support dispatch documentation.

The design challenge

Making Stock In
make sense.

My biggest challenges: parent–child items and item-level quality checks.

One of the hardest parts of Stock In was figuring out how to add parent and child items and make their relationship clear throughout the flow. I needed to show where each child item belonged without losing the quantity and unit information needed for receiving. Item-level QC was another challenging part: quality-check information had to stay connected to the correct item as the transaction moved through review.

  • Make the parent–child relationship visible.The challenge was both adding child items and showing their connection to a parent across the Stock In flow. The final table uses shaded parent rows, numbered child rows, and item actions to make the hierarchy visible alongside quantities and units.
  • Keep QC connected to the item.Item-level quality checks added another layer of complexity. The design separates the transaction’s QC choice from item-specific QC Failed and Deviation quantities, with an added-items summary to review those entries before confirmation.
  • Make review a complete workflow.A draft, an approval-pending transaction, and a sent-back record require different actions. Attachments, remarks, and decision controls need to stay connected to the transaction being reviewed.

Design process

Understand. Explore. Refine.

The work moved from operational requirements and workflow structure to detailed forms, review screens, and exception states.

  1. Understand

    Requirements & workflow mapping

    Break down each transaction into its source, required information, item details, documents, and review actions. Map the main steps alongside draft, approval, and send-back states.

  2. Explore

    Wireframes & layout exploration

    Explore the sequence of lists, entry forms, previews, and details. The original wireframes establish the relationship between transaction information, item tables, notes, and actions.

  3. Refine

    Detailed states & iteration

    Develop the shared application shell, grouped form sections, item drawers, and review controls. Work through feedback and interaction details before preparing the designs for handoff.

Key workflows

One operation.
Three connected workflows.

Each module has its own business information, while the shared structure keeps related tasks recognizable.

Stock In

Receiving starts by identifying the source: a purchase order, other documents, or a gift. For PO receipts, the form connects the order reference and challan details with the received date, receiver, and QC choice.

The parent–child item flow was a particular challenge for me. I worked through how items could be added and how their relationships would appear across the flow. In the final table, shaded parent rows anchor numbered child rows beneath them, with add actions on parent rows and edit or remove actions on child rows. Quantities and units remain in the same table, so the relationship and receiving details can be read together.

Solution & client acceptanceI resolved the parent–child item flow, and the client accepted the solution. The hierarchy is visible in the table, while item-entry details have their own drawer. A separate QC/deviation drawer supports item-specific quality-check information.

Stock Out

The outgoing-stock form distinguishes Issue with Documents from Damage. For an issue, the transaction captures the destination project or department, branch, recipient, and supporting reference.

The displayed charge-from-program choice is followed by GL and MIS code fields. Item entry brings present stock and issue quantity into the same table, with attachments and remarks supporting the record.

Design decisionEstablish the transaction’s purpose and destination before asking for items. Keep stock context beside the quantity being issued.

Purchase Orders

The PO detail screen links the vendor, order date, approver, currency, and transaction status to the ordered items. Item details include project, lot, unit, quantity, receiving values, and costs.

A separate delivery-schedule table holds delivery date and location. Keeping schedule information separate gives it a clear place without mixing it into the order summary.

Design decisionShow order identity and status first, then item-level detail and delivery commitments. Provide a Preview action from the same record.

Reflection

A clear screen needs
a clear business model.

Working on an enterprise application taught me to look beyond the main form and follow the transaction through its different states.

A quantity only makes sense with its unit and transaction context. An approval action only makes sense when the record is ready for review. A sent-back transaction needs a way to explain the correction and continue.

Solving the parent–child item flow taught me to check a relationship across the whole journey, not just in one table. The client’s acceptance was an important milestone. Working through item-level QC also reinforced the need to keep exception details tied to the right item and transaction.
